Cutting Europe forecast error from 25% to 10%

Company
Rayence, medical x-ray detectors
Role
Global Sales Strategy & Analytics
Period
09/2025 to present
Tools
Excel, weekly reporting

Context

Rayence sells detectors to about 20 international accounts. The annual sales forecast decides how much to produce, so every point of error becomes either idle stock or a late order.

Problem

Forecast error in the Europe division was 25%. The team needed a forecast it could trust for production planning and a way to see misses early.

Approach

  1. Analyzed three years of x-ray detector sales data across 23 companies to build annual forecasts.
  2. Compared forecast with shipments every week for Europe, so a miss showed up while there was still time to adjust.
  3. Published weekly performance reports with Excel charts against annual and quarterly targets.
  4. Benchmarked competitor specifications and sales to spot market opportunities.

Outcome

Europe forecast error fell from 25% to 10%.

The weekly reports gave the team one shared view of progress against annual and quarterly targets.

What I took from it

Talking with overseas customers every day showed me that a forecast records how customers behave. It moved my question from how large the error is to why a customer orders when they do.

Reading 240+ customers’ feedback next to the molding line

Company
LG Chem, Ravenna, Ohio
Role
Global Sales Operations Analyst
Period
06/2024 to 07/2025
Output
Dashboards, monthly KPI reports

Context

I supported sales operations for automotive customers. Two sources described the same product from different sides: what customers said, and what the process records showed.

Problem

Colour matching and injection molding came up again and again in customer feedback, and the process records showed recurring discrepancies. The task was to find out whether they were the same problem.

Approach

  1. Built and maintained dashboards for roughly 500 manufacturing process records a month to surface recurring injection-molding errors.
  2. Read feedback from 240+ customers and grouped complaints into recurring pain points around colour matching and molding.
  3. Worked with 3 Midwest automotive vendors and 6 Latin American companies to clarify requirements and coordinate technical follow-ups.
  4. Combined feedback, defect data and sales trends into monthly KPI reports and presented the findings to internal laboratory teams.

Outcome

Recorded discrepancies dropped from 45 to fewer than 10.

Customer service issues decreased. A separate demand analysis contributed to a 15% increase in raw material purchase orders.

What I took from it

This is where data stopped being a report and became a way to hear users. It is the reason I want formal research methods behind my analysis.

Auditing 300+ library records before visualizing them

School
Chung-Ang University
Role
Team lead, data visualization project
Data
Library records in an Oracle dataset
Tools
Tableau

Context

A university data visualization project built on library records, the kind of material my degree is about.

Problem

A chart is only as reliable as the records under it, and the Oracle dataset had missing data. Before anything could be visualized, someone had to decide how to handle the gaps.

Approach

  1. Evaluated more than 300 library records one by one.
  2. Identified missing data in the Oracle dataset.
  3. Compared five preprocessing techniques on the same records.
  4. Visualized the results in Tableau.

Outcome

Tableau views built on records the team had audited and preprocessed.

What I took from it

A ten-page paper on the limits of the Dewey Decimal Classification showed me that a fixed hierarchy struggles with new and interdisciplinary subjects. The same instinct made me check a dataset’s structure before charting it.
